Sleep Sack TOG Guide: What TOG Rating Do You Need?

TOG explained · room temperature chart · updated August 2026

TOG is the single most useful number on a sleep sack label — and the one most parents don't understand until they've bought the wrong sack twice. Here's what it means, what to wear under it, and the room temperature chart worth pinning to the fridge.

What TOG actually means

TOG (thermal overall grade) measures how much warmth a fabric holds in. The higher the number, the warmer the sack. There's no official standard every brand follows, but the industry roughly agrees:

0.2–0.5 TOG — a muslin-weight layer for hot summer nights. 1.0 TOG — the standard all-rounder for normal room temperature. 2.5 TOG — winter weight. 3.5 TOG — very cold rooms only. Anything marketed as "all-season" (like merino sacks) is doing the job of several TOGs at once, which is why it costs more.

The room temperature chart

Red Nose Australia advises that 18–20°C is a comfortable nursery temperature, and the TOG bands below follow the guidance printed by major sack brands (Tommee Tippee, Love To Dream and Ergobaby all publish near-identical charts). Dress baby in one layer under the sack — the sack does the rest.

Room temperatureTOG ratingWhat baby wears underneath
Above 24°C0.2–0.5 TOGShort-sleeve bodysuit (or just a nappy)
21–23°C1.0 TOGShort-sleeve bodysuit
18–20°C2.5 TOGLong-sleeve bodysuit
16–17°C2.5 TOG + layer, or 3.5 TOGLong-sleeve bodysuit with legs covered
Below 16°C3.5 TOGLong-sleeve bodysuit — and consider heating the room rather than piling on layers

Two rules of thumb: when in doubt between two TOGs on a mild night, choose the lighter one — overheating is the greater risk. And if the room sits above 24°C, skip the sack entirely; a bodysuit alone is safer than any sack.

How to layer safely

Check baby's temperature at the chest or back of the neck — it should feel warm, not hot or sweaty. Cool hands and feet are normal and don't mean baby is cold. Sweating, a flushed face, or rapid breathing are the signs to strip a layer off.

Never add a blanket on top of a sleep sack — that recreates the loose bedding a sack exists to replace. And never put a sack over a swaddle; once baby starts rolling, move to a sack with arms free or a transition sack.

Overheating warning: overheating is a recognised SIDS risk factor, and it's the reason TOG matters at all. If you're unsure whether baby is too warm, err on the cool side — a slightly cool baby sleeps fine; an overheated one is in danger. Red Nose Australia's guidance: room around 18–20°C, baby on the back, head uncovered, no loose bedding.

Merino vs cotton: the all-season question

Merino sacks (like the Woolino) are marketed as all-season, and the claim mostly holds: wool wicks moisture and buffers temperature swings, so one sack covers everything from an 18°C winter night to a 22°C spring one. Cotton sacks hold heat instead of moving it, which is why they need a TOG per season — fine, but it means owning three sacks and guessing which one fits tonight's forecast. Merino's catch is price and care: it needs gentler washing than cotton, and some babies find wool itchy against bare skin (the Woolino's lining handles this for most).
