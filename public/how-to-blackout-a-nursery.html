How to Blackout a Nursery (2026)

the 3-second test · mounting high & wide · rental-friendly options · updated August 2026

Daylight is the enemy of a 5am summer wake-up: light suppresses melatonin and tells a baby's brain it's morning long before it should be. You don't need expensive custom blinds — most nurseries can be made genuinely dark with a rod, a panel and ten minutes. Here's the full DIY sequence.

Step 1: The three-second test

Close the curtains, shut the door, kill the lights — then hold your hand in front of your face. If you can still make it out after three seconds, light is leaking in somewhere. Do the same test at 5am in midsummer, because that's the light that's actually waking your baby. The three-second test tells you whether you have a problem; finding the leak is usually just a matter of following the light.

It's also worth being honest about whether you need full blackout at all. Some babies sleep fine with curtains half-closed; others treat a streetlight like an alarm clock. If yours is the second type, proceed.

Step 2: Mount high and wide

The single biggest upgrade is geometry, not fabric. Mount the rod 10–20 cm above the window frame and extend it 15–30 cm past each side. This blocks the top gap (where morning light pours in) and the side gaps (where edge light sneaks around the panel). Buy curtain width at roughly 1.5–2× the window width so the fabric bunches and seals instead of stretching flat.

This one change fixes most nurseries by itself — before you spend anything on "blackout" fabric, check whether your current curtains are simply mounted wrong.

Step 3: Close the gaps — valance and side seals

If light still leaks over the top of the rod, add a valance — a fabric skirt mounted above the rod that drapes down over it. For serious light (west-facing rooms in summer), a pelmet box — a U-shaped wooden or MDF casing around the top of the window — blocks everything from above. Cheap magnetic or Velcro tape along the sides of the window seals the edge gaps the fabric alone won't.

For renters who can't drill above the frame: tension rods inside the recess, or adhesive-backed blackout panels that stick to the frame and peel off cleanly at move-out.

Portable and temporary options

  • Blackout travel kits — suction-cup or magnet panels that go up in any hotel room; worth it if you travel with a baby who sleeps light.
  • Blackout liner panels — hang behind existing curtains; the rental-friendly middle ground.
  • Window film / cling — darkens glass itself; great for doors and odd-shaped windows, harder to remove cleanly.
  • A spare fitted sheet over the rod — the classic no-spend hack; it works surprisingly well.
Safety first: corded blinds are a strangulation hazard — Red Nose Australia recommends cordless or tied-up cords well out of reach. Blackout fabric changes light, not sleep safety: baby still sleeps on the back, on a firm flat surface, with no loose bedding, and a dark room is no reason to skip the 18–20°C room temperature check.

When blackout backfires

A minority of babies wake earlier in total darkness, because light is part of the body clock's "morning" signal. If blackout makes things worse, don't fight it — crack the curtains 10 cm at wake-up time so day and night read differently, or use a wake-up lamp as the morning cue. The goal isn't a cave; it's a room where darkness and daylight mean different things.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 3-second blackout test?

After installing, stand in the nursery at noon and close the curtains. If you can see your hand clearly, there's a light leak — fix the edges before buying anything fancier.

How high should blackout curtains hang?

As high as the ceiling allows, and 15–20cm past the window frame on each side. The gap over the top of the rod is the most common leak — a pelmet or valance fixes it.

What's the cheapest way to blackout a nursery?

A pleated paper shade (like Redi Shade) at roughly $20 installs with zero tools and blocks light at the window itself. It's not pretty — but it's honest, and it works.
